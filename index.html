Why this tab exists

This tool's claim is that it lowers an AI-detector score. That is only checkable if a detector is in the loop — so here are the two honest ways to check it, and the reason neither is automatic.

No commercial detector (GPTZero, Originality, Turnitin, Winston, QuillBot) is validated on any Indian language, and none offers a usable free API. Several also refuse browser calls outright. So the loop is driven by the local calibrated scorer, and verification against a real detector is recorded here — as a pair. A lone “after” number is evidence of nothing.

Record a before / after by hand

Copy each text into the detector of your choice, then type both numbers in. The delta is the only reading that means anything, so a row stays incomplete until both halves exist.

A generic adapter. It runs at most twice per rewrite — once on the input, once on the final output — and never inside the loop: five passes would be ten paid calls, and a vendor's number should not be steering edits in a language it was never validated on. Left unconfigured it refuses to run rather than guess where the score lives in an unknown response.

The honest version

Lowering this score does not guarantee GPTZero, Turnitin or Originality will call your text human. They are closed, they disagree with each other, and none is validated on any Indian language. What this measurably does is remove the specific artefacts — measured against real text — that make copy read as machine-produced.

The score comes from signals calibrated on real Hindi: native BBC Hindi, human-translated Wikipedia, and raw machine translation, separating at AUC 1.00 / Cohen's d 3.2. English has its own separately measured bands. Those are the only two calibrated languages. For the other 21 the word lists are genuinely per-language and the literal matches stay reliable, but the numeric bands are Hindi's extrapolated — so for those the loop optimises defect count, not the number, and no verdict label is shown.

What the loop is allowed to chase

Chasing a score invites edits that move the number without improving the text. Two signals are therefore measured and reported but never optimised:

SignalWhy it is not chased
Passive voice (English, weight .15) Its bands were measured on long articles; short text scores 0 regardless of who wrote it, and the band was never retuned. Chasing it means inserting passives to harvest points from a measurement that does not apply.
Colon-led lists (English, weight .25) The detector matches a literal colon. Swapping “:” for an em dash kills the match without changing a single word — a pure exploit. A model may rewrite the list into prose; the offline pass may not.

The English objective is renormalised over the remaining three signals (.353 / .353 / .294). The four Indic signals keep the equal .25 weights they were calibrated with.

How a pass is judged

The score chooses the direction and picks the winner. It is never allowed to grant admission. Every candidate clears these in order, and evaluation stops at the first failure — so a good score can never rescue a candidate that failed a gate.

#GateRejects
1Placeholders & figuresA dropped protected value, or a figure the model invented. Every number, price, dosage, URL, email and phone is replaced by an opaque token before the model sees the text, so an invented figure is a string comparison rather than a judgement call.
2Protected spansA URL, dosage or price that changed, checked against the original.
3MeaningA hedge that was dropped — “may reduce” quietly becoming “reduces”.
4LengthDrift beyond 75–125% of the original and ±15% per pass. Both, because three chained 12% cuts pass a per-pass check and still lose a third of the text.
5Locked termsAny term you listed that vanished.
6ScriptIndic text drifting back toward English.
7Metric gamingCommas deleted without sentences being split; stub paragraphs inserted to spike variance.
8Defect tradingA pass that fixes two defects by creating a third.
9ScoreOnly now: no meaningful gain, no acceptance.

A rejection does not end the run — it discards that candidate and retries. The output is the best-scoring accepted pass, and the free offline pass is always on that leaderboard, so a run where every model attempt is rejected still returns a real improvement rather than your raw input.

A measured failure of the English score, kept here on purpose

A real 1,392-word document scored 8.8% — “reads as human-written” here, while QuillBot called it 96% AI-generated and GPTZero 74%. The tool was wrong, not the detectors.

The cause is structural, not a fluke. tell_1k and colon_lists_1k are 55% of the English weight between them and are keyword lookups — against “moreover”, “delve into”, “tapestry”. Current model prose does not use that 2023 vocabulary, so both returned a perfect 100 for the absence of it, which is not evidence a person wrote anything. Only passive_ratio fired, and that is the one signal deliberately kept out of the objective — so the loop had nothing to act on and ran zero passes.

Measured across every labelled English fixture available, the current composite ranks the human sample as more AI-like than the AI mean (39.8 vs 33.3). Signals that do separate: nominalisation density (AI 55.6/1k vs human 12.0), contractions (zero in all five AI fixtures, 4.8 in the human one), and first/second person (10.0 vs 64.7).

Those signals ship as observations, not scores — displayed, and fed to the rewriter, but carrying zero weight. There is only one human fixture available, and one sample cannot set a band; inventing calibrated-looking numbers is the exact mistake that produced an AUC of 0.21 the first time this detector was built. To promote them, eight or more genuinely human-written English documents are needed.

Meanwhile the English verdict no longer claims “human-written” when the score came mostly from absent keywords, and for English you should check a real detector — unlike the Indic languages, English is well served by GPTZero and QuillBot, so there is no excuse for relying on the proxy.

Importing a blog from a URL

A browser cannot fetch an arbitrary website directly — almost no site sends an Access-Control-Allow-Origin header, so the request is blocked before it starts. Getting round that needs a proxy, and a proxy sees the URL you asked for.

So the order is deliberate: a direct read is tried first every time, and when it works no third party is involved at all. Only if the site blocks it does the tool fall back to r.jina.ai, then allorigins.win. Which route succeeded is printed next to the result, every time. It never silently proxies.

Two consequences worth knowing. Expect red console errors on a blocked direct read — the browser emits “blocked by CORS policy” itself, before any script sees it, and no page can suppress that. Seeing it means the fallback is working. And fetched page content is data, not instructions: it lands in the textarea for you to look at, and if you then run a rewrite it is sent to your chosen model as text to edit. Read what you loaded before spending a call on it.

Sites behind Cloudflare, a login or a paywall generally cannot be read this way. Paste the text instead.

Keys, cost and privacy

There is no backend. Your text goes from this page straight to whichever provider you picked, using your own key, which is stored in this browser's localStorage and nowhere else. No key ships with the tool and none is ever defaulted. The offline pass and all scoring need no key and make no network call at all.

Run the regression suite with node test.mjs, or open test.html. Both run the same assertions offline against scripted model output, at zero cost.

Static page, no backend, no build step. The detector's calibrated constants come from the measurement record in bhasha-seo/CALIBRATION.md and are pinned by a regression test, so a band cannot be quietly tuned to make a demo look better.

Providers and keys

Keys are held in this browser only. Unconfigured providers stay listed and disabled rather than hidden — a hidden provider looks like one that does not exist. Model names are editable defaults, not pins: provider model ids churn, and a stale default should be a thirty-second fix.
